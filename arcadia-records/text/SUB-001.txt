SECTION 1
The administrative object and governing comparison
The object before Arcadia is a pair of protected-tree permits. TRH 26-11 seeks authorization to remove 34 protected oaks 
associated with the proposed cross-country course. TRE 26-09 seeks authorization for improvements and course activity 
within the protected zones of 45 additional oaks. The City page also identifies 34 new trees. [1] The regulatory question is 
therefore narrower and more disciplined than a referendum on the Olympics. Arcadia must decide whether the applicant has 
carried the burden imposed by its tree-preservation regime for these specific removals and encroachments.
Arcadia treats protected trees as significant ecological and aesthetic resources for present and future residents. A healthy-tree 
application must explain why removal is necessary, why removal is more desirable than alternative project designs, and what 
mitigation is proposed. The regulations apply a comparable alternatives inquiry to protected-zone encroachments. They also 
define protected zones broadly enough to capture grading, hardscape, parking, construction, storage, and other activity that 
can affect roots, and set a minimum zone of 15 feet from the trunk while requiring inclusion of known root structure. [2, 3]
Those provisions establish a design hierarchy. The project should first move around the protected resource. Removal becomes a 
candidate only after the applicant has identified feasible alternatives, applied disclosed criteria consistently, and demonstrated 
why redesign does not solve the conflict. A preferred route, a faster construction schedule, a more convenient broadcast plan, 
or a more commercially useful post-Games course may be relevant project considerations, but none becomes a necessity 
finding simply by being included in the chosen design.
The comparison cannot stop at one earlier, worse route
Santa Anita's vice president of equestrian operations stated that an earlier course affected more trees and was modified to 
reduce environmental impact while preserving safety. [4] That statement is useful because it confirms that the course geometry 
is adjustable. It does not establish that the present alignment is the least damaging feasible option. The relevant denominator 
is not “the selected route compared with the worst route previously considered.” The relevant denominator is the full field of 
reasonably feasible alternatives, including routes designed from the outset with protected trees treated as immutable 
exclusions.
A credible alternatives record should present materially different alignments side by side. Each should show route geometry, 
surveyed tree and protected-zone impacts, grades, drainage, footing transitions, obstacle logic, emergency and veterinary 
access, operational effects, broadcast and spectator constraints, cost, schedule, temporary and permanent works, and post-
Games use. Each rejection should identify the mandatory criterion that caused it. Without that comparison, the City cannot 
distinguish true sporting or safety constraints from accumulated preferences.
CONTROL QUESTION
What competent evidence demonstrates that removal of these 34 protected oaks, and encroachment upon 45 more 
protected zones, is necessary and more desirable than every reasonably feasible lower-impact design?

SECTION 3
Site capacity: why a zero-removal study is reasonable
Santa Anita is not a constrained neighborhood parcel. FEI describes a 320-acre facility with a 1,100-foot grandstand seating 
26,000, an infield capable of accommodating 50,000 people, 61 barns for more than 2,000 horses, and an on-course veterinary 
clinic. [5] Santa Anita separately advertises more than one million square feet of flexible indoor and outdoor event space and 
describes its vast parking lots as a core filming and event asset. [9, 10] These facts do not prove that every acre is usable for 
Olympic cross-country. They do establish a level of site capacity that makes a tree-exclusion design study rational and 
proportionate.
The property has also received recent equestrian capital investment. In 2024, 1/ST installed three major infield show arenas: a 
150 by 300 foot main arena, a secondary arena totaling almost 140,000 square feet, and a 125 by 250 foot schooling arena. The 
same announcement stated that temporary stabling would be placed in the north parking lot and described the investment as 
part of a strategy to transform the infield into a world-class equestrian venue. [6] Santa Anita also completed an $8 million, six-
furlong Tapeta training oval to complement its one-mile main dirt track. [8]

This is an important allocation of responsibility. Residents should not be told that they must produce a certified Olympic 
course before asking the applicant to redesign. A resident can identify an obvious capacity question. The regulated actor must 
then demonstrate, with the technical record it controls, why the capacity cannot be converted into a compliant lower-impact 
alternative.
SECTION 4
ZR-1: hardscape-first, tree-exclusion feasibility hypothesis
ZR-1 classifies the design problem as a constrained route-synthesis exercise. Its first input is not the preferred course line. Its 
first input is the surveyed exclusion layer: every protected trunk, known root structure, and legally defined protected zone. 
That geometry is treated as fixed. The route, obstacles, temporary surfaces, access plans, and spectator functions must move 
around it unless the study demonstrates that a specific mandatory criterion cannot be satisfied.

Sector A: north parking and hardscape capacity
The north parking field should be evaluated as a temporary engineered competition sector rather than as background land

modular topography, portable obstacles, temporary drainage capture, protected crossings, and post-event restoration on 
selected hardscape lanes.
A serpentine or folded geometry can create substantial course distance within a broad parking field without requiring a 
permanent single-loop landscape. The design team should model approach lines, speed control, turning radii, horse sightlines, 
obstacle spacing, heat, glare, dust, drainage, and emergency bypasses. If a hardscape sector fails, the report should identify the 
measured failure and the mitigation tested, rather than rejecting the concept through a general assertion that cross-country 
“needs grass.”

SECTION 9
Replacement accounting and ecological time
The City project page identifies 34 removals and 34 new trees. [1] Arcadia's tree-preservation regulations state a default 
minimum of two new 24-inch-box trees for each protected tree approved for removal, while allowing the Director to modify, 
waive, increase, or reduce the replacement requirement. [2] Thirty-four removals therefore begin from a default of 68

container size, planting location, irrigation, establishment period, mortality replacement, long-term maintenance, projected 
canopy, habitat assumptions, and time to defined performance milestones.

SECTION 10

SECTION 11

Santa Anita's history supplies evidence of institutional adaptability, but it must be used with care. The site was converted in

eventing endurance and cross-country phase was staged at Fairbanks Ranch rather than at Santa Anita. Recent investments in 
the Tapeta oval and infield arenas provide a modern example of deliberate site adaptation. [6, 8]

Native spreadsheets, geospatial files, presentations, drawings, schedules, and email families with attachments are materially 
more useful than flattened screenshots. Plans should be provided at original available resolution. The City should identify a 
records coordinator, tracking number, determination date, estimated production schedule, and any proposed narrowing 
before treating a category as unduly burdensome. [24, 25]
